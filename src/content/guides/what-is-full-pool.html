When a lake page says a reservoir is "2.4 ft below full pool" or "86% full", both numbers depend on how the operator divides the reservoir into zones. This guide explains those zones, why the same lake can be quoted in different datums, and why percent full and feet below full pool tell you different things.

A lake level is an elevation, not a depth

A reported lake level is the height of the water surface above a fixed reference, called a vertical datum. The Texas Water Development Board (TWDB) defines water surface elevation as "the elevation of the top surface of a reservoir's body of water" (TWDB glossary). So a level of 681 ft does not mean the lake is 681 ft deep. It means the surface is 681 ft above the datum, which is close to sea level. To know whether the level is high or low, you compare it with the lake's own reference levels, above all full pool.

The three main zones of a reservoir

Most reservoirs that store water are divided, from the bottom up, into three zones.

Dead pool

TWDB defines dead pool as water "that cannot be drained by gravity through a dam's outlet works" (TWDB glossary). It is not counted as usable storage.

Conservation pool

The conservation pool is the storage above dead pool and below the maximum normal operating level. TWDB calls the top of this zone the conservation pool elevation and treats it as the same thing as the maximum normal operating level (TWDB glossary). It is the water held for the lake's authorized uses, such as water supply, hydropower and recreation. A U.S. Army article from the Corps' Fort Worth District describes the top of conservation pool as "the lake's normal or target operating level" (U.S. Army, Sept. 4, 2026).

Full pool, on this site, means the top of the conservation pool or the operator's equivalent term. For example, the Lower Colorado River Authority says Lake Travis "is considered full for water supply purposes at 681 feet msl" (LCRA).

Flood pool

On lakes built for flood control, the flood pool sits above the conservation pool. TWDB defines it as water "that lies above the conservation pool in reservoirs with flood storage function" (TWDB glossary). The Fort Worth District article explains that "flood storage is reserved to capture runoff from storms and reduce flooding downstream", and that the lake stores part of a flood and releases it later under its water control plan (U.S. Army). Lake Travis is the only one of the six Highland Lakes designed to hold back floodwater, and LCRA operates it during floods under Corps of Engineers regulations (LCRA: dams and lakes; LCRA: key elevations).

So a level above full pool on a flood-control lake is not "extra water supply". It is floodwater being held temporarily, which the operator plans to release. On this site such a lake is labelled "Above full pool", and "In flood pool" once it is 2 ft or more above full pool.

Full pool goes by several names

Operators use different words for the same idea: conservation pool, normal pool, full pond or summer pool. Some lakes also follow a guide curve, a target level that changes through the year. The Corps' Hydrologic Engineering Center describes the guide curve as "the target or desired pool elevation for the reservoir", with flood control storage above it and conservation storage below it (USACE HEC). On a lake with a winter drawdown, being several feet below summer full pool in winter can be normal. Each lake page prints the operator's own term for full pool and notes when the lake follows a seasonal target.

Why the datum matters: NGVD 29 and NAVD 88

Elevations are measured from a vertical datum, and two national datums are in common use. The National Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1929 (NGVD 29) was tied to local mean sea level at 26 tide stations. The North American Vertical Datum of 1988 (NAVD 88) replaced it. According to the National Geodetic Survey, the difference between heights in the two datums in the lower 48 states ranges from −40 cm to +150 cm, depending on location (NOAA NGS).

In lake data:

  • TWDB states a datum for each Texas lake. Lake Travis uses "NAVD88 +0.6"; Canyon Lake uses NGVD29 (TWDB: Travis data file; TWDB: Canyon).
  • USGS uses different parameter codes for the same measurement in different datums: 62614 is lake elevation above NGVD 1929 and 62615 is above NAVD 1988 (USGS parameter codes).
  • TWDB notes that its historical levels were adjusted to one datum, so they can differ from older figures on a partner's site (TWDB FAQ).

The practical rule: only subtract a full-pool value from a level in the same datum. If one website quotes a lake in NGVD 29 and another in NAVD 88, their numbers can differ by a foot or more without either being wrong. This site stores the datum with every lake and prints it in the "About this reading" block.

Percent full is about storage, not height

TWDB defines percent full as "the ratio of conservation storage to conservation capacity expressed as a percentage" (TWDB glossary). It is a volume measure. TWDB turns a water level into a volume with the lake's elevation-area-capacity (rating) curve, built from surveys and bathymetric models (TWDB methodology). Because the relation between level and volume comes from the shape of the lake bed, a lake that is 50% full is usually not halfway down from full pool in feet, and a 1 ft drop can mean a large or small share of storage depending on the lake and the level.

Three more details matter when you read percent full:

  • It stops at 100%. TWDB caps conservation storage at conservation capacity and does not count flood pool storage (TWDB: Canyon Lake footnotes). A lake holding floodwater still shows 100%. Use feet above full pool to see how high it is.
  • Dead pool is left out. TWDB subtracts dead pool storage from both the storage and the capacity (TWDB methodology).
  • Shared lakes may use one side's share. For Amistad and Falcon on the Mexico border, and for Caddo, Texoma and Toledo Bend, TWDB's percent full uses Texas's share of the capacity (TWDB: statewide).

Capacity also changes as sediment builds up, and TWDB updates its curves from new surveys (TWDB: data providers), so percent full can differ between agencies for the same day.

How this site uses full pool

  • Full pool comes from the same agency as the level when that agency publishes it (TWDB's conservation pool elevation, or a Corps district's top of conservation). Otherwise it comes from the operator's own page, stored with its link, datum and the date we checked it.
  • The level label uses percent full when the source gives it, and feet below full pool when it does not. The thresholds are on Data sources and methods.
  • A lake with no reliable full-pool value shows its level and change with the label "Level only".
